3Why it's happening

Imagine a bouncer who's told: "stop anyone wearing a hat." Easy rule to follow — but tons of totally normal people wear hats too. Now half the line gets stopped for nothing.

That's what your models did. Hacker text often has lots of symbols — quotes, semicolons, brackets. But so does completely normal text — addresses, error messages, code. The models learned "lots of symbols = bad" instead of learning what an attack actually looks like.

4What we already tried

We fed the models more normal sentences that also had symbols in them, hoping they'd learn the difference. It helped a little — false alarms dropped from 80% to about 73% — but the core problem is still there. More examples alone can't fully fix it.

6What to do next

  1. Give it smarter clues. Instead of just counting symbols, teach it to notice the pattern — like a quote right before "OR 1=1", not just a quote anywhere.
  2. Let both models work together. That's literally why your thesis uses a team of models instead of one — where the Random Forest guesses wrong, the LSTM might catch it, and vice versa.
  3. Test on your real evasion set. The AI-generated attacks (from Ollama) are the true test of your thesis. That's the number that really matters.
  4. Don't panic about the 73%. This is a normal, useful thing to discover — and honestly, it's a great thing to write about. It shows you understand your system's limits, not just its wins.

Bottom line

Your models are genuinely great at spotting real trouble. They just need a bit more practice telling regular smoke from an actual fire. That's a normal step in building AI security tools — and it's a much more honest (and interesting) thing to put in your thesis than a suspiciously perfect 100%.
